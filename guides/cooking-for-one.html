Cooking for one without it feeling like a chore

Cooking for one feels like a chore because the effort is the same as cooking for four and the reward is smaller. The fix is to change the maths. Cook two or four portions at once and keep a short rotation of meals you like. Then treat eating on your own as something you do for yourself, not something you’re missing.

By the Just Cooked teamUpdated 7 November 20263 min read

Try this first

  1. Cook double, on purpose. Most meals scale with no extra effort. Eat one, box one for tomorrow or the freezer. Future you is a person worth cooking for.
  2. Keep a rotation of six. Six meals you like, that you can make without thinking. Rotate them. Add a new one when you fancy it.
  3. Shop for the week you’ll actually have. Not the one where you cook every night. Two or three cooked meals, plus easy fallbacks, is honest for most weeks.
  4. Buy small, freeze smart. Bread, meat and bagged veg in the freezer stop the waste that makes cooking for one feel expensive.
  5. Sit down and eat it properly. At a table if you have one, with a drink you like, not standing at the counter. It takes the same ten minutes and feels completely different.

Why it feels like a chore

Cooking for other people has a built-in point. Cooking for yourself can feel like effort with no audience, and on a tired evening that tips easily into toast or a takeaway. There’s also the waste: packs sized for families, half an onion going soft, bread you can’t get through. None of this is a character flaw. It’s a set of small frictions, and each one has a small fix.

Change the maths

The effort of cooking doesn’t double when the portions do. A traybake for four takes the same twenty minutes as a traybake for one. So cook for two or four and let the extra portions become tomorrow’s lunch or next week’s freezer dinner. Suddenly one evening of cooking feeds you three times, and the nights you can’t face it already have an answer.

Make waste disappear

Buy things that keep: eggs, tins, pasta, rice, frozen veg, frozen fish. Freeze bread and take out slices as you need them. Chop the whole onion and freeze what you don’t use. Choose recipes that use the same few fresh things in different ways across the week. Cooking from what’s already in is the single biggest saver, and there’s a whole guide on that.

Make it nice

You’re allowed to cook something you love just because you love it. Music on. A proper plate. The good hot sauce. Eating alone can be one of the calmest parts of a day if you let it be a thing you do for yourself rather than a reminder of who isn’t there. And if the quiet is the hard part, eating while on a call with a friend is a perfectly good dinner.

If the lonely part is the real problem

Sometimes “I can’t be bothered to cook for myself” is really “I don’t feel worth the bother”, and that’s a different thing from logistics. It’s common and it’s worth saying out loud to someone. Small meals at regular times still help in the meantime, and our guide on eating regularly when it feels hard is there for the days it gets heavy.

Questions people ask

How do I stop wasting food when I live alone?
Buy things that keep, freeze bread and leftovers, use the same fresh ingredients across several meals, and cook from what’s already in before you shop. Cooking double and freezing half turns waste into next week’s dinner.
What are good meals to cook for one person?
Anything that scales or keeps: traybakes, soups, curries, chilli, pasta bakes, fried rice, omelettes. Cook two or four portions and freeze the rest, or pick one-pan meals that are quick enough not to need leftovers.
Is it normal to not want to cook when you live alone?
Very. The effort feels bigger when there’s no one to share it with. Making it easier, with a short rotation and a freezer of backups, usually helps more than trying harder. If it’s tied to feeling low, that’s worth talking to someone about.

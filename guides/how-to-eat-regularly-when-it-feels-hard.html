How to eat regularly when it feels hard

Eating regularly when it feels hard works best when you make it smaller and more routine: something at roughly the same times each day, even if it’s tiny, rather than waiting until you feel like a meal. Appetite often follows eating, not the other way round. And if it’s been hard for a while, you deserve support with it, not just tips.

By the Just Cooked teamUpdated 7 November 20263 min read

Try this first

  1. Anchor to times, not hunger. Hunger signals go quiet when you’ve been skipping. Pick three rough times and eat something at each, hungry or not.
  2. Make it small. A slice of toast. A banana. A yoghurt. Half a sandwich. Small and eaten beats big and skipped.
  3. Remove the steps. Keep food that needs no preparation within reach: crackers, cheese, fruit, cereal bars, nuts, a smoothie from the shop.
  4. Set a quiet reminder. A phone alarm that says “something small” is enough. Not a lecture.
  5. Don’t make up for missed ones. If you miss lunch, that’s all that happened. Eat the next one.

Why it gets hard

Stress, low mood, a busy brain, illness, grief or a run of bad weeks can all switch appetite off or push food to the bottom of the list. The longer meals get skipped, the quieter the hunger signals get, which makes it feel like you don’t need to eat. You do. Your body just stopped shouting about it.

That’s why waiting to feel hungry doesn’t work well here, and why going by the clock does.

Small counts

On the hard days, the goal is not a balanced dinner. It is something in your body at roughly regular times. A piece of toast at eight, a yoghurt and a banana at one, some crackers and cheese at six is a day of eating. It keeps the rhythm going, and rhythm is what brings appetite back. When a better day comes, you can add to it. You don’t have to earn that.

Make eating need nothing from you

Every step between you and food is a place to stop. So remove them. Buy things that are ready to eat. Keep some where you’ll see them. Put a bowl and a spoon out the night before. If cooking is the wall, our guide to easy meals when you have no energy has a list of meals that need almost nothing.

When a moment gets heavy

Sometimes the hard part isn’t the food. It’s what comes up around it. If a mealtime brings a wave of panic or dread, slow your breathing first: in for four, out for six, for a minute. Then do the smallest possible thing, like a few bites, and let that be the win. Having someone to message or call in those moments helps more than any technique, so it’s worth deciding who that is while you’re feeling okay.

If it’s been hard for a while

Skipping meals most days, losing weight without meaning to, or feeling frightened of eating are all reasons to talk to someone, and none of them are too small. Your GP is a good first call. Beat, the UK eating disorder charity, helps anyone who’s worried about their eating, whether or not it has a name. You don’t need to be sure something is wrong to ask.

When it’s more than a hard day

If food has felt hard for a while, or you’re skipping meals most days, it’s worth telling someone. Your GP is a good first call. In the UK, Beat helps anyone who’s worried about their eating, whether or not it has a name. Samaritans are there any time on 116 123, free, for whatever is going on. NHS 111 can help when it’s urgent but not an emergency, and in an emergency you call 999. Outside the UK, a doctor and your local emergency number are the right first calls. Questions people ask

Why do I forget to eat?
When meals get skipped, hunger signals fade, and stress or a busy mind can drown out what’s left. It isn’t carelessness. Eating by the clock rather than by hunger, with small ready-to-eat foods close by, is the most reliable way back.
What should I eat when I have no appetite?
Something small, soft and easy: toast, yoghurt, a banana, a smoothie, soup, crackers and cheese. A few bites often brings appetite with it. Eat at regular times rather than waiting to feel hungry.
Is it an eating disorder if I skip meals?
Not necessarily, but if it’s most days, if you’re losing weight without meaning to, or if eating feels frightening, it’s worth talking to your GP or to Beat. You don’t need a diagnosis to ask for help. Just Cooked isn’t a medical or therapy app, and it can point you toward people who are.

Guides · Eating disorders

I think I might have an eating disorder. What do I do?

If you think you might have an eating disorder, you don’t need to be certain, and you don’t need to look a certain way, to ask for help. Tell one person you trust, book a GP appointment, and contact Beat, the UK eating disorder charity, who support anyone worried about their eating. Reaching out early tends to make things easier, and nothing about asking commits you to anything.

By the Just Cooked teamUpdated 7 November 20264 min read

Where to start

  1. Take the worry seriously. If food, eating or your body are taking up a lot of your headspace, that matters on its own. You don’t need to tick a list of symptoms.
  2. Tell one person. A friend, a parent, a partner, a teacher. You don’t have to explain it well. “I’m struggling with food and I don’t know what to do” is enough.
  3. Book a GP appointment. Say you’re worried about your eating and you’d like to talk about it. You can take someone with you, and you can write down what you want to say beforehand.
  4. Contact Beat. Beat’s helpline, webchat and online groups are there for anyone worried about their eating, at any stage, including before you’ve spoken to a doctor.
  5. Be gentle with yourself in the meantime. Regular meals, no numbers, no punishment for a hard day. None of this is your fault.

You don’t have to be sure

A lot of people wait because they think they don’t count. They aren’t thin enough, or ill enough, or it hasn’t been going on long enough. None of that is how it works. Eating disorders affect people of every size, age, gender and background, and most of the people who have one don’t look the way films suggest. If food has become a source of fear, control, shame or constant calculation, that is reason enough to ask for help.

Signs worth taking seriously

It isn’t a checklist, and having one or two of these doesn’t decide anything. But if several sound familiar, it’s worth talking to someone:

  • Food or your body takes up a lot of your thinking, most days.
  • You have rules about eating that feel hard to break, or that you’d be ashamed to tell someone about.
  • You eat in a way that feels out of control, and then feel awful about it.
  • You skip meals, avoid eating with other people, or hide what you do or don’t eat.
  • Your mood, energy or ability to concentrate have dropped alongside changes in how you eat.
  • Someone who cares about you has said they’re worried.

Telling someone

The first conversation is usually the hardest part, and it doesn’t need to be a good conversation. Pick someone who’s kind rather than someone who’s clever. You can say it in a message if saying it out loud feels impossible. Something like: “I’ve been struggling with food and it’s getting bigger than I can handle on my own. I don’t need you to fix it. I just needed to say it.”

What to say to a GP

You can ask for a double appointment if you think you’ll need the time. Write down the things you want them to know, because it’s easy to shrink it in the room. Say plainly that you’re worried about your eating and you’d like to be referred for support. If a GP dismisses you, you’re allowed to ask to see a different one. Beat has guidance on preparing for that appointment.

While you wait

Support can take time to arrive, and the waiting is hard. What helps in the gap is small and unglamorous: eating something at regular times, even when it’s tiny; keeping numbers out of your life where you can; having one person who knows; and not punishing yourself for a bad day, because a bad day is part of this, not evidence against you. If a moment gets frightening, slow breathing helps the body settle, and the helplines are there at any hour.

Just Cooked can sit alongside all of that, but it isn’t treatment and it isn’t a substitute for it. It can hide numbers, give you a simple meal when deciding is too much, and put the people and helplines you choose a tap away. The professional support is the part that changes things.

When it’s more than a hard day

If food has felt hard for a while, or you’re skipping meals most days, it’s worth telling someone. Your GP is a good first call. In the UK, Beat helps anyone who’s worried about their eating, whether or not it has a name. Samaritans are there any time on 116 123, free, for whatever is going on. NHS 111 can help when it’s urgent but not an emergency, and in an emergency you call 999. Outside the UK, a doctor and your local emergency number are the right first calls. Questions people ask

Do I need a diagnosis to get help for an eating disorder?
No. Beat supports anyone worried about their eating, and a GP can refer you for assessment based on what you tell them. You don’t need to prove anything or fit a category to deserve help.
Can I have an eating disorder if my weight is normal?
Yes. Eating disorders happen at every size, and most people with one are not underweight. How you feel about food and what it’s doing to your life matter far more than any number.
Can an app treat an eating disorder?
No, and be careful of any that claims to. Just Cooked isn’t a medical or therapy app. It can make daily eating gentler, hide numbers and put support a tap away, alongside the care of a GP, a therapist or a specialist team.
Who can I contact today?
In the UK, Beat’s helpline and webchat are there for anyone worried about their eating. Samaritans are on 116 123, free, at any hour. Your GP is the route to assessment and treatment. In an emergency, call 999.

How to support someone who’s struggling with food

The most useful thing you can do for someone struggling with food is to stay close without taking over. Say what you’ve noticed, once, kindly. Don’t comment on their body or what’s on their plate. Keep inviting them to ordinary things. And encourage them towards professional help, because love is necessary but it isn’t treatment.

By the Just Cooked teamUpdated 7 November 20263 min read

Start here

  1. Pick a calm moment, not a mealtime. Say what you’ve noticed and that you care. “I’ve noticed you seem to be finding food hard lately, and I’m here” is enough.
  2. Listen more than you talk. They may deny it, get angry or go quiet. That’s common. You’ve opened a door, and it stays open.
  3. Leave bodies and plates out of it. No comments on weight, shape, how much they ate or didn’t. Even compliments can land badly.
  4. Keep life normal. Invite them to things that aren’t about food. Watch something together. Go for a walk. Connection helps more than monitoring.
  5. Encourage professional help, gently. Offer to help book the GP, or to come along. Beat supports carers too, and you’ll need support of your own.

What helps

People who are struggling with food usually already know. What they often don’t have is someone who can be near it without panicking, controlling or looking away. That’s the job. Be the person who stays steady: who asks how they’re doing rather than what they ate, who notices the hard days without making them a drama, and who keeps treating them as a whole person rather than a problem.

Practical help counts too. Shopping together, cooking something simple alongside them, sitting with them after a meal when that’s the hard part, or just being on the end of a message at a difficult time of day.

What to avoid

  • Comments on bodies, theirs or anyone’s, including “you look well” or “you look healthy”. These get heard in ways you don’t intend.
  • Policing food. Watching plates, counting, bargaining, or praising them for eating. It turns meals into a test.
  • Simple fixes. “Just eat” and “just stop” are not available to them, or they would have done it.
  • Making it about you. Your fear is real, but they can’t carry it. Take it to someone else.
  • Ultimatums. They tend to end the conversation, not the problem.

Mealtimes

If you share meals, aim for calm and predictable. Decide the plan beforehand rather than negotiating at the table. Talk about anything other than the food. If a meal goes badly, let it go and try again at the next one. If you’re supporting someone who is in treatment, their team may give you specific guidance on mealtimes, and that guidance comes first.

Getting them to help

You can’t make an adult see a doctor, but you can make it easier: offer to find the GP’s number, to sit in the waiting room, to help them write down what they want to say. Beat, the UK eating disorder charity, has a helpline and resources for people who are worried about someone else, as well as for the person themselves. If they are a child or a teenager, you can go to the GP yourself to ask for advice.

Looking after yourself

Supporting someone through this is tiring and frightening, and it can go on for a long time. You’re allowed to find it hard. Talk to someone outside the situation, use Beat’s support for carers, and keep some of your life that isn’t about this. You’ll be more use to them steady than exhausted.

When it’s urgent

If they faint, have chest pain, or you’re worried about their immediate safety, call 999 or go to A&E. If they talk about not wanting to be here, take it seriously: Samaritans are on 116 123 at any hour, for them and for you.

When it’s more than a hard day

If food has felt hard for a while, or you’re skipping meals most days, it’s worth telling someone. Your GP is a good first call. In the UK, Beat helps anyone who’s worried about their eating, whether or not it has a name. Samaritans are there any time on 116 123, free, for whatever is going on. NHS 111 can help when it’s urgent but not an emergency, and in an emergency you call 999. Outside the UK, a doctor and your local emergency number are the right first calls. Questions people ask

What should I say to someone with an eating disorder?
Say what you’ve noticed and that you care, then listen. Avoid comments about their body or their food. “I’m here, and I’m not going anywhere” does more than any advice.
Should I make them eat?
No. Pressure and policing tend to make things worse. Keep meals calm and predictable, eat together when you can, and leave the plan to them and, if they have one, their treatment team.
Where can I get help as the person supporting them?
Beat supports carers as well as people with eating disorders, through its helpline and online groups. Your own GP can help you too. You don’t have to do this on your own.

Stress and food: eating too much, or not at all

Stress changes how you eat, and it goes both ways: some people reach for food to settle the feeling, others lose their appetite completely. Both are normal responses to a body under pressure, not failures. What helps is the same either way: regular meals as a buffer, a way to settle in the moment, and no punishment afterwards.

By the Just Cooked teamUpdated 7 November 20263 min read

In the moment

  1. Name it. “I’m stressed and I want to eat” or “I’m stressed and I can’t eat”. Naming the feeling takes some of the drive out of it.
  2. Breathe before you decide. In for four, out for six, for a minute. Then decide about food. The decision is usually calmer.
  3. If you can’t eat, go small. A few crackers, a banana, a yoghurt, a milky drink. Something is better than nothing, and appetite often follows.
  4. If you want to eat, eat something, at a table. Put it on a plate. Sit down. Taste it. Eating on purpose is different from eating to escape, and it tends to stop sooner.
  5. Don’t make up for it. Skipping the next meal to balance things out keeps the cycle going. The next meal is just the next meal.

Why stress does this

When you’re under pressure, your body changes its priorities. For some people that switches appetite off: there’s no room for food when everything feels urgent. For others, food becomes the quickest way to feel briefly better, because it works, for about ten minutes. Which way you go is mostly temperament and habit. Neither is a weakness, and both tend to get worse the longer the stress goes on without a break.

The buffer: regular meals

The single most useful thing, in both directions, is eating at roughly regular times whether or not you feel like it. If stress kills your appetite, regular small meals keep your energy and mood from dropping through the floor, which is what makes stress feel unmanageable. If stress makes you eat, regular meals mean you’re rarely truly hungry when the urge hits, and the urge is easier to notice for what it is. Three meals and something in between, kept simple, is the whole plan.

In the moment

A stress urge peaks and passes. Breathing slowly for a minute, in for four and out for six, gives it time to pass and gives you a gap to choose in. If you then still want to eat, eat, deliberately: a plate, a seat, your attention on it. Eating that way satisfies something. Eating standing at the fridge while scrolling doesn’t, which is why it keeps going. If you can’t eat, make it tiny and easy and don’t argue with yourself about it.

Afterwards

Whatever happened, let it be what it was. Guilt is the thing that turns a stressful evening into a bad week, because it drives the skipping and the secrecy that keep the cycle alive. You ate more than you meant to, or less than you needed. That’s information about how stressed you are, not a verdict on you.

When it’s more than stress

If eating has become the main way you cope, if it happens in a way that feels out of control, or if it’s followed by shame, hiding or making up for it, that’s worth talking to someone about. Those patterns are common and they respond well to support. Your GP is a good first call, and Beat, the UK eating disorder charity, helps anyone worried about their eating, whatever it looks like.

When it’s more than a hard day

If food has felt hard for a while, or you’re skipping meals most days, it’s worth telling someone. Your GP is a good first call. In the UK, Beat helps anyone who’s worried about their eating, whether or not it has a name. Samaritans are there any time on 116 123, free, for whatever is going on. NHS 111 can help when it’s urgent but not an emergency, and in an emergency you call 999. Outside the UK, a doctor and your local emergency number are the right first calls. Questions people ask

Why do I eat when I’m stressed?
Eating works, briefly: it settles the body for a few minutes, so stress learns to reach for it. Regular meals, a pause to breathe before you decide, and eating deliberately at a table all weaken the reflex. Guilt afterwards strengthens it.
Why can’t I eat when I’m anxious?
Anxiety switches appetite off. It’s a normal stress response, and it usually eases as the stress does. Small, easy foods at regular times keep you going in the meantime. If it lasts weeks, talk to your GP.
Is stress eating a problem?
Not on its own. Everyone does it sometimes. It becomes worth attention when it feels out of control, happens often, or is followed by shame and making up for it. That’s when it’s worth speaking to a GP or to Beat.

Eating well on a tight budget

Eating well on a budget comes down to a short list of cheap staples that make real meals, cooking from what you already have before you shop, and letting almost nothing go to waste. It doesn’t need coupons or spreadsheets. And if the money for food itself is running out, there is help, and asking for it is not a failure.

By the Just Cooked teamUpdated 7 November 20263 min read

Try this first

  1. Build on cheap staples. Eggs, oats, rice, pasta, potatoes, lentils, tinned tomatoes, beans and tuna, frozen veg, onions. Most good meals come from these.
  2. Shop from the cupboard first. Look at what you have, plan around it, then buy only the gaps. A lot of what gets thrown away was bought while something similar sat at home.
  3. Cook once, eat twice. Double the pan and freeze half. The second meal costs almost nothing and saves a takeaway on a bad night.
  4. Go frozen and tinned without guilt. Frozen veg and fish are as nourishing as fresh, cheaper, and they don’t go off. Tins are proper food.
  5. Plan for the tired nights. Keep two ten-minute meals in stock. Takeaway is where the budget actually goes, so this is the step that saves most.

The staples that do the work

You don’t need a big shop. A small set of cheap ingredients covers most meals: eggs, oats, rice, pasta, potatoes, lentils and dried or tinned beans, tinned tomatoes, tinned fish, frozen peas and mixed veg, onions and garlic, oil, stock cubes, and a few things for flavour like soy sauce, curry powder or a jar of pesto. Add some cheese and whatever fresh veg is cheap that week and you can cook for a month.

Meals that cost very little

  • Lentil or bean soup: an onion, a tin of tomatoes, a stock cube, a handful of lentils. Feeds you for two days.
  • Egg fried rice: yesterday’s rice, an egg, frozen peas, soy sauce.
  • Pasta with tomato sauce: a tin of tomatoes, garlic, oil. Add beans or tuna to make it a full meal.
  • Jacket potatoes: with beans, cheese, tuna or whatever is left.
  • Chilli or curry: onions, a tin of beans, a tin of tomatoes, spices. Cheap, filling, freezes well.
  • Porridge: oats and milk or water. The cheapest good breakfast there is.

Waste is the hidden cost

Food that gets thrown away is money you spent twice: once at the shop and once when you replace it. The fixes are small. Freeze bread and leftovers. Cook from what’s already in before buying more. Keep the things that go off at the front where you’ll see them. Our guide on cooking with what you have is built around this.

The takeaway leak

For most people on a tight budget, the money doesn’t go on groceries. It goes on the nights nothing was planned and a takeaway was the only option that needed no energy. Two ten-minute meals kept in stock plug that leak better than any amount of careful shopping. There’s a guide for that too.

If the money itself is the problem

Sometimes the issue isn’t how to spend the food money well. It’s that there isn’t enough. If that’s where you are, please don’t go hungry quietly. In the UK, Trussell food banks can help right away, and Citizens Advice can check what support you’re entitled to. Many areas also have community fridges and pantries. Asking is not a failure. It’s what those places are for.

Questions people ask

What are the cheapest healthy foods?
Eggs, oats, rice, pasta, potatoes, lentils, beans, tinned tomatoes, tinned fish, frozen vegetables and onions. They keep, they’re filling, and between them they make most meals.
How can I eat well on very little money?
Build meals from cheap staples, cook from what you have before shopping, cook double and freeze half, and keep two quick meals in for the nights you’d otherwise order in. If there isn’t enough money for food at all, food banks and Citizens Advice are there to help.
Is frozen veg as good as fresh?
Yes. It’s frozen soon after picking, it’s usually cheaper, and it doesn’t go off in the drawer. For most meals it’s the better buy.
